## What we talked about

Digivizer is two businesses stapled together, a SaaS product and an agency,
which sounds messy but is actually pretty useful. The agency folks are hitting
real customer problems every week, so the product team never gets to drift off
building metrics nobody asked for.

Most of the conversation ended up being about the year we'd just had. Our
customers had moved a long way from the Microsoft and Lenovo end of town toward
smaller Australian businesses, a lot of them on Shopify or some blend of retail
and online. The gap we closed was website analytics. Folks could see exactly
what they'd spent on paid, and then nothing at all about how many sales came
out the other side. Joining that up end to end was the missing piece, and it
quietly became the thing that won demos.

On remote work, engineering barely noticed. We were already spread between
Sydney, Bathurst and Melbourne and already collaborating online, so not much
changed. The teams who'd always sat together at desks felt it much harder. The
real casualty was onboarding, because the informal version of onboarding is
sitting near people and pairing constantly, and that just evaporated overnight.

We reopened the office in early 2021 and almost nobody came in! Turned out what
people wanted it for was workshops: book a room, stand at a whiteboard, go home
again. So when the building changed hands we let the permanent office go, took
shared space about a day a week, and put on a monthly all-hands with food and
drinks. Being around people matters even when the work doesn't strictly require
it.

Then a good chunk on ensemble programming, which is pair programming taken up
to team size. VS Code's collaboration plugin does the heavy lifting, and
pleasingly it lets me keep my vim bindings while everyone else types like a
normal person. We used it to onboard two juniors we'd hired in April and June
2020, mid-pandemic, and the thing that made it click was picking genuinely
non-trivial problems. Trivial ones just dispirit people. Two seniors and two
juniors on one problem, seniors driving until they hit a fork, then opening the
trade-off up to the floor. Rising tide, all boats: the juniors ask the
clarifying questions, and the seniors have to go back and check what they'd
assumed. Three or four hours in an afternoon is about my limit before I'm
wrecked. Both juniors went permanent, so two for two.

Hard agree on hiring juniors, by the way. Skip them for four or five years and
you'll wonder where your mid and senior pipeline went. One of ours came from
CS and the other from a science background (analytical, coded at uni, loved
it), and that difference in how they come at a problem is the whole point.
Stack a team with similar seniors and you get similar output and similar blind
spots. The trait worth recruiting for is curiosity. If you can find folks who
are genuinely curious about how things work and who've already proven they can
learn to code, we can teach them the rest.

There's a nice university angle too. Our software ended up being used in a
digital marketing course at a Sydney uni, and one marketing intern found us
that way and came looking. Beats a job ad.

And the obvious objection, which is a fair one: aren't you paying two people to
solve one problem? Yes, and on paper it looks slower. It stops looking slower
once you count true cost of ownership, the debugging, the changed requirements,
the production issues. Solving it early is cheaper than solving it in prod, and
the customer gets a better product either way. Writing juniors off as too hard
is short term thinking the moment you're past being a three person startup.
